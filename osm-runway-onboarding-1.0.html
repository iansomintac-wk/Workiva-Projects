New since the original tour

Three workflows
the tour didn't cover.

Everything before this chapter assumed a live, post-implementation account. These three cover what happens before Go-Live, what your partner already wrote down, and the fifteen minutes after a call ends.

Before Go-Live

The implementation phase

While the partner builds the workspace, the OSM manages the partner relationship, scope, and timeline — not sessions. The tracker models this as a pre-journey: Align → Kick-Off → Build → UAT → Go-Live, and only then the session curriculum you practiced above.

  1. Flag the account. Add Customer → “Still in implementation”, or Edit Account → Move into implementation for a record you already track.
  2. Manage it from the Implementation tab. Phase stepper, partner firm and contact, target Go-Live date, and a risks list with severities you resolve as the build progresses.
  3. If the customer goes quiet, pause the implementation. The pause is open-ended, takes a reason, and surfaces in Today's implementation strip with days elapsed — with zero session or Gainsight nagging attached.
  4. Mark Go-Live when the build ships. The full tracker activates and the pacing clock re-anchors to that day, so four months in Build never reads as 120 days stalled.
Tracked, not nagged. In implementation there is no risk badge, no health score, no session triage, and no Gainsight to-dos. The trade is a Today strip that never hides: paused builds, open high risks, and flagged partner notes stay visible until Go-Live.
Partner data

Partner sheet import

Partners keep their own trackers in Workiva sheets. When you get a copy, import their notes and calls instead of retyping them.

  1. Settings → Import Partner Notes. Upload the .xlsx or .csv copy of the partner's tracker.
  2. Confirm the column guesses. The importer proposes which columns hold the customer name, the note, and the date — correct them before anything is staged.
  3. Review the staged rows. Exact name matches arrive pre-checked. Near-misses stay unmatched with a per-row account dropdown — you decide that “Beta Limited” means “Beta LLC”, not a fuzzy matcher. Likely duplicates arrive unchecked and labeled.
  4. Import. Each confirmed row becomes a Partner Sync activity with a provenance line naming the source file and import date. Re-uploading next month's copy is safe — anything already imported flags as a duplicate.
Nothing writes until you confirm. Every row stages for review first, and an imported note is always distinguishable from one you typed.
After the call

The Session Debrief

Log the call, attach the transcript, state what you couldn't answer live — and get verified answers plus a customer-ready recap email. Requires your own AI key in Settings.

  1. Log the call with its transcript. Paste or attach it in Log Activity; it stores with the activity and rides exports and archives.
  2. Type your outstanding questions in the debrief card, one per line. They are stated by you — never inferred from the transcript.
  3. Generate. You get coverage gaps, quote-verified score suggestions you accept or reject one by one, and answers grounded in this customer's own curriculum — including sessions they haven't reached yet.
  4. Copy the drafted recap email. Curriculum-grounded answers cite “from your onboarding curriculum”; everything else is flagged as needing confirmation. Review, sign, send.
Verification is mechanical, and privacy is explicit. Citations must quote-match the actual curriculum text or they are discarded and counted; an answer with no surviving citation is labeled unverified general knowledge — never send those unchecked. Unlike the rest of the tracker's AI, the debrief sends your real note and transcript text to your configured provider, and the card says so every time.

Also new since the original tour: AI Call Prep on each account's Overview tab now builds a full context-switch synopsis with next actions and escalation routing (Climate Consultant, PSM, partner team, Solution Architect), and the Progress Report button in the header exports a manager-ready audit of the whole book.

Added since this guide was written

What the
tour skips.

The tracker has kept moving. These are the additions that change the daily loop rather than sitting in a corner — each is one screen away and none of them replace anything you have already learned.

Between calls

Quick log — press L

Logging used to mean opening the account, the panel, then the form. Press L anywhere outside a text field, pick the account, say what happened, Ctrl/Cmd+Enter. It builds the same Gainsight checklist the full form does, so nothing is skipped by using the fast path.

Why it matters: every signal in the tracker is derived from logged activity. When logging slips, the advice gets quietly worse while looking exactly as confident.

First thing

Your calls are the fixed points

Today opens with a line stating the day — how many calls you have to prep, how many accounts need you. Then the timeline itself, and your calls sit in it at the moment they happen rather than in a separate list.

Scroll and the panel keeps pace. Reach a call and it brings up the recap of your last meeting, where the account stands, every question still open across the whole history, what is still unchecked, and what this session is meant to produce. You can log the call from there without leaving.

Set the time and the day does the arithmetic. A movement between two timed calls states what it actually measured — 3 items · roughly 30 min · about 30m free between 9:30am and 11:00am (allowing 60 minutes a call). The hour-per-call assumption is printed rather than hidden, so you can disagree with it and know exactly which number to change.

Questions persist. Anything you capture in a Session Debrief is kept against that conversation, so it reappears before the next call instead of living in your memory.

The work

The day, in the order it happens

Today is one timeline, and the axis is your workday. Your calls are the fixed points. The ranked accounts fill the gaps between them, and the admin gathers at the end. You work it once, top to bottom, and the day is over.

One account gets one beat. If Northstar has a call today, an open signal, and a Gainsight item waiting, that is a single stop carrying all three — not three rows in three places. The severity band still labels each beat, and the first time a band appears it tells you how to read it: Needs you today is the only one where a person asserted something — a flag you set, a promise you made. Everything below it the tracker inferred, so read the note before you act.

The clock is optional, and it is earned. Set a time on a call and the day is measured in real minutes, with work fitted into the actual gaps. Leave it unset and the movements stay ordinal — before your first call, between calls — because the tracker will not print a time it does not have.

The panel beside it is not a link to the work — it is the work: create the Success Outcome Plan, add the CSAT contact, capture sentiment, queue a CTA. Everything writes to the same record the account tabs use, and everything you queue collects in the close, so one push at the end of the day clears Gainsight instead of interrupting you five times.

Two densities, one day. Story gives each beat the screen in turn and follows your scroll — it is how you walk a day you have time for. Compact puts the same fifteen beats on about half the page so you can see all of them at once, which is what you want with four minutes between calls. In Story you scroll, click, or press J and K; in Compact you click or press J and K, because at that height several beats share the screen and scrolling should not keep changing your mind for you. The choice sticks until you change it.

It also remembers where you were. Come back after a call and the day opens on the beat you left, not back at the top — unless that account has dropped out of today, in which case you start at the beginning again, which is correct. Anything the day cannot hold falls into a list underneath, and it says what pushed it there. There is no “start here” badge — the day starts where you are.

Coordination

People, blockers, commitments

Each account has a Coordination tab: who is involved and in what role, what is waiting on someone else and since when, and what was promised in either direction.

These feed Today. A commitment past its date ranks at the top alongside a flagged note — those are the only two things a person explicitly asserted; everything else the tracker infers. A blocker open seven days or more surfaces on its own. Most onboarding delay is somebody else’s turn, and until it is written down nobody can count it.

The week

Copilot reads the whole book

Today answers “what now”. Copilot answers “what next”, and it has a time axis: the rest of this week, then the month ahead, then your book read as one thing.

The week is a diff, not a list — who joined Needs Attention since the last snapshot, who came off, who came back from pause. On your very first visit it says it has no baseline yet rather than inventing one. The month answers what a week cannot: what is projected to land inside 30 days, what sits outside the ±21-day graduation SLA, and which live accounts still have no committed date at all.

Every count carries its accounts. “Eight accounts have gone quiet” is useless if you have to go and work out which eight, so the names are right there and each one opens.

Then the work splits in two, because two kinds of work behave differently. Administrative batches: when the same gate is missing on several accounts, do them in one sitting. Strategic does not: when several accounts independently hit the same wall, that is one content problem, not five coaching problems. An optional AI second read sits at the end — it never runs by itself.

Risk

Decide it is real before you work it

The tracker raises risk signals from six places — a note you flagged, a keyword it matched, pacing that slipped, a taxonomy phrase, an implementation record, a committed date it will miss. Some of those are real and some are the tracker misreading a sentence.

Every signal for one account is one case. A flagged note, an Off Track pace and a matched keyword on the same customer are a single decision, not three — they arrive under Needs review, and you read the evidence, then answer one question: is this a real risk? Nothing asks you for an owner, a target date or a summary until you have said yes.

Say yes and the case opens: what to do, who is driving it, a 300-character executive summary you can paste straight into a CTA, and Copy for Gainsight. Say no and it files as a false positive with your reason attached. That is not a permanent silence — it comes back on its own if the account deteriorates past the point you dismissed it, or after 30 days, because the reason you dismiss something is usually “not this week” rather than “never”.

The summary is drafted locally and instantly; Sharpen with AI rewrites it from the account’s notes if you have a key. The CTA deliberately leaves owner and target date blank — those belong to Gainsight, and filling them here would be inventing them.

Handoff

Both ends of the lifecycle

A Handoff tab records what was sold coming in, and composes the knowledge-transfer packet going out to the CSM. The facts — people, session history, open blockers, commitments, risks — are assembled from the book every time, so the packet cannot go stale. You only write the part the data cannot know: what worked, what is fragile, what to watch.

Provisional: this field set is a placeholder until the internal format is confirmed. Anything you write is preserved when the fields change, and every export is stamped as a working draft, not an official document.

Safety

Undo, and backing up

An Undo button appears in the header after any change and reverts it; pressing it again puts the change back. It is one level deep and expires after ten minutes, on purpose — an undo you no longer remember is a trap.

The export reminder beside the account count is a button: click it to download a backup. The book lives only in this browser and none of it exists in Gainsight, so that reminder is the most important control on the screen.

End of day

Wrapping up the day

The bottom of Today is the close. It groups what you queued today by kind, and links to the Gainsight section where you review it. Wrap up the day closes today once and shows two counts: the beats you walked and the items still queued for Gainsight.

Beside it, Back up the book says how long ago you last exported. Nothing in the tracker estimates minutes saved.

Gainsight

The queue, and what it does not do

Anything you queue — an outcome plan draft, an at-risk CTA, a batch of to-dos — collects in one list on the Risk tab. You can open any item to read the exact text, remove it, or export the whole queue as JSON.

There is no Gainsight transport, and the tracker says so rather than implying otherwise. The tab says so in the queue’s own note rather than implying a push, and the close on Today repeats it. Nothing is ever sent silently. Today the queue is a staging area: paste from it, or hand the JSON export to middleware. A future backend fills one function and the same queue starts pushing — which is why it is worth using now.

Storing Gainsight credentials in Settings does not change this. It readies the seam; it does not open it.

Under the hood

The tracker is still marking its own homework

Every time you press Done or Not this on a recommendation, and every time you confirm or dismiss a risk, the tracker records which signal it was and why. It builds a per-signal scorecard from that: how often you act, how often you wave it away, and the most common reason you gave.

You will not see it, and that is deliberate. It used to have a panel on Copilot called “Is the advice any good?”. That is a question for whoever tunes the thresholds, not something an OSM needs between calls. The data still accrues; it just no longer takes up room on a surface you use to plan a week.

What it means for you is simple: dismissing a false positive is safe. It does not quietly down-weight that whole class of signal, because the reason you dismiss something is usually “not this week”, not “never relevant”.

In flow

Clear Gainsight items without leaving the account

The Account Brief on an account’s Overview lists that account’s outstanding Gainsight items inline, and lets you tick one off where you are — while you are already thinking about that customer, not in a separate admin sitting.

The Gainsight To-Do tab still exists and does a different job. The brief is the in-flow surface: clear one item in passing. The tab is the batch surface: review everything before a Gainsight catch-up, then take it in one action with Copy all & queue, which copies every pending item as paste-ready text and stages it in the queue at the same time.

Same list, two moments. Use whichever matches what you are doing.

The panel

Where it stands, not the note it stands on

A logged note is often long — a real one runs to ten key points and seven next steps. Today does not show you the note. It shows you where the account stands, in three layers.

Five counted facts: last touch, sessions completed, whether first value is evidenced, how many questions are open, how much has been logged at all. Then the shape of the relationship — 4 interactions over 77 days · about one every 26 days · 2 Onboarding Sessions, 1 Check-in, 1 Email · 1 unchecked item · 1 flagged. Cadence tells you something a single note cannot: whether you are actually in touch with this customer.

Then a short extract of the last touchpoint, in your own words, labelled with how much more there is: Extract of your note (142 words). Read the full note. It is a truncation, not a generated summary — the tracker will not hand you its own reading of a note you wrote, because that would be one more thing you had to check.

The note itself lives in the Activity Log, which is where a full note belongs. What reaches Today is a short quote of what you flagged — enough to recognise it, not enough to bury everything underneath it.

Open questions come from your whole history with that account, not just the last call. A question raised three sessions ago and never answered is exactly the kind of thing that goes missing, so it stays on the panel until you mark it handled.

Draft an answer

Any open question has a Draft an answer button. The answer is written to your own instruction — you set it once in Settings → How AI should answer customer questions, and it controls tone, length and format. The tracker does not invent a house style for how you talk to customers.

What your instruction cannot do is loosen the grounding. The answer is built from that customer’s own onboarding curriculum first. Anything that has to come from general knowledge is labelled as such and carries a line saying it needs verifying before the customer relies on it. Nothing about Workiva behaviour is invented, whatever the instruction asks for.

It saves as a draft on the question, and the tracker tells you to read it before you send it. That is the whole contract: it gets you to a first sentence, it does not speak for you.

Context

AI call prep, and how much of the history it read

On any account you can ask for a synopsis that gets you back into the relationship without re-reading every note — what has happened, where it stands, what to raise.

It tells you what it was built from. If the account has more logged activity than the synopsis could take in, the first line says so: built from the 20 most recent of 34 logged activities. If the response hit its length cap it says that too, and invites you to regenerate rather than quietly ending mid-thought.

That line is the point. A synopsis that silently read half the history and sounded complete would be worse than no synopsis, because you would trust it.

Upward

The progress report your manager can read

One button assembles the whole book into a standalone HTML file you can send or print: every account grouped by what it needs, the longest-waiting float to the top of each group, with stage, health, sessions completed, days since the last touchpoint, why it landed in that group, and the recommended next action.

It carries the portfolio time-to-first-value figure and the count of outstanding Gainsight items.

It is a snapshot, not a live link. Nothing in the file updates after you export it, and nothing leaves your machine unless you send it. Generate it the morning of the conversation, not the week before.

Before you commit

How many sessions this customer actually needs

The session count used to be a number you typed, and weekly-versus-biweekly was a standing argument. Both are worked out for you now. On Edit account, tick the solution components the customer actually bought — Financial Reporting, any of the four GRC components, Sustainability, CSRD, Carbon. The Session Plan then shows the arithmetic: Core 3 + Sustainability 2 + CSRD 3 + Carbon 2 = 10 sessions.

Then it answers the cadence question. Thirteen weeks holds 13 weekly slots or 6 biweekly ones, so a 10-session plan fits weekly with three spare and misses biweekly by four. You get that verdict at plan time rather than discovering it in week ten. Worth knowing before your first call: every single-component customer fits biweekly except Controls Management, and no combination does.

Where the window comes from. If the account has both a Go-Live and a committed graduation date, the fit is measured against the real gap between them and says so. Without them it falls back to the 13-week standard and says that instead — a fit against an assumed window is a weaker claim than one against a date you committed to, and the panel never lets the two look alike.

It will tell you when it disagrees with you. If the account is set to 8 sessions and the components add to 10, it says so plainly: one of the two is wrong, and it does not guess which.

Where the weights came from. Workiva’s own published onboarding paths, measured as solution-specific eLearning minutes, with the shared platform training lifted into Core so it is counted once rather than per solution. CSRD is the exception — Workiva publishes no CSRD path, so its weight is inferred and carries an asterisk everywhere it appears. Every weight is editable in Settings with its basis printed beside it, because these size content; they do not measure how you deliver. Change them as you learn what your sessions actually hold.

Booking

Put the next session on a real calendar

Two fields on Edit account do more than they look like. Next session scheduled on is the date you actually booked — it feeds the scheduling gap Today ranks on, so an account with a session coming up and nothing booked surfaces before one that is already sorted. Leave it blank when it genuinely is not booked; a guess here makes the ranking worse, not better.

Time on the calendar is what turns your day from a running order into real minutes. With it set, Today measures the gaps between your calls and fits work into them. Without it the day stays ordinal — before your first call — rather than claiming a precision the book does not have.

Then Add to calendar hands you the invite. It downloads an .ics you open into Google Calendar. With a time set it is a 60-minute event at that hour; with only a date it is an all-day entry. It does not invent the hour: if the date came from your pace rather than from a booking, the invite stays all-day whatever time is stored, because that session was never actually scheduled.

It will not offer you a date that has already gone. On an account that is overdue, the paced date is in the past, so the invite is dated today instead. A date you booked yourself is never moved, even into the past — that is your record of what happened, not an estimate the tracker gets to correct.

The download tells you exactly what it made, and which of those reasons it used. Read that line before you send it: it is the difference between an invite for the session you booked and one for the day the tracker thinks you are due.

There is no calendar integration behind this, in either direction. Nothing syncs, nothing is read back, and booking a session in Google does not tell the tracker. The .ics is a file you carry across — the same honest seam as the Gainsight queue. Re-importing after you change the hour usually updates the same event rather than creating a second one, because the invite keeps a stable ID.

Orientation

Risk is a deck. Copilot is a journey.

The two tabs deliberately do not move the same way, because they are not the same job. Risk is a pile you work down. Copilot is a distance you travel along.

Risk deals you one case at a time. The account you are deciding sits in front of you as a card; the ones still waiting are stacked physically behind it, and a line underneath says how many. As you scroll, the front card lifts away and the next comes forward. The stack is the backlog — how much is left is something you see rather than something you count.

The rail on the left is the map of that queue, and its colour is the point. Each row carries its account’s severity as a bar, and the rows sit flush so those bars form one continuous column: a block of red, then amber, then grey. When ten of seventeen cases are severity 3, a badge on every row tells you nothing and that column tells you everything.

Cases are ordered by what still needs a verdict, hardest first, so [01] is always the next thing to look at. Press J and K to move between them — faster than scrolling — and click any row in the rail to jump straight there.

Copilot moves through time instead, and it moves in layers. Month marks drift slowly in the background, the graduation marks travel at middle speed, and the writing keeps pace with you — the way distant things move slower than near ones when you are travelling. Each chapter says when it is about before it says what: Now — the next few days, then Thirty days out, then standing back at the whole book.

The marks running down the left are real: one for each account projected to graduate inside thirty days, placed at its date. A cluster of them is a cluster of work landing at once. Nothing projected in that window means no marks at all, and it says so in a sentence rather than drawing an empty ruler.

Neither tab hides anything behind the motion. Every risk case still shows its evidence and its one question, and every Copilot chapter is on the page whether or not you have scrolled to it. If you have reduced motion turned on, both tabs drop the movement entirely and render as plain lists — that is a real fallback, not a slower animation.

The next 30 days

Copilot opens on the stretch you are working

The tracker is called Book of Business — Runway, and both halves do work now. The book is the container: each tab carries a chapter numeral, 01 Today through 04 Copilot. The runway is each account’s journey — already how the tracker talks, in landings, on track and projected graduation.

Copilot opens on a band of the next thirty days. Every account projected to graduate inside that window sits on it at the day it lands, green if it is inside the ±21-day tolerance against its committed date and red if it is not. Click any mark to open the account. A cluster of marks is a cluster of graduations arriving together — the thing a list of names cannot show you.

The lit stretch follows what you are reading. While you are on The rest of this week it covers the next seven days; move down to The month ahead and it opens to all thirty. The band itself never moves, so the marks stay where you learned them.

The AI read sits on the band, because asking for help planning is a question about that stretch of time. It never runs by itself, and it sends pseudonymized account data to your own provider using your own key. The answer appears further down, under Ask AI for a second read.

On Risk, every case carries a glideslope — the small scale beside the severity readout. The path is marked at the top and one lit marker shows where the account sits against it: severity 1 is on path, severity 4 is at the bottom. The deck of cases descends toward a runway threshold drawn beneath it.

First hour

Catch the book up in one sitting

Almost every number the tracker produces is downstream of two acts: logging a call, and marking a session complete. Until those exist it has nothing to measure, so it falls back to an assumed pace and every projection quietly becomes the same guess wearing a different date.

Settings → Backfill Sessions is one grid instead of thirty panel visits. A row per live account, how many sessions are actually done, the date of the most recent one, and optionally the next one you have booked. Tab across, save once.

It only ever adds. A number lower than what is already logged is treated as a typo and refused by name, never obeyed. You enter one date; earlier sessions are spaced evenly and each is marked as an estimate in its own note, because a date the tracker invented should say so.

Read it right

When it says Assumed, believe the word

An account with no completed sessions has no pace to measure, so its projected graduation is today plus the remaining sessions at the default. The tracker used to print that as Est., which claims a reading it does not have.

It now says Assumed, in a dashed tag, with the reason in the tooltip. If several accounts show the same assumed date, that is not a coincidence — it is the same arithmetic run on the same missing data.

Today also opens with one line naming the largest gap in the book and the control that closes it. Dismiss it and it moves to the next gap rather than disappearing.

When it goes wrong

Plays: a sequence, not a single action

The things that actually go wrong in onboarding are not one-action problems. An account going quiet is: reach the champion, reach around them, reach the sponsor, decide — over two weeks. The failure is never “I did not know”. It is “I did step one and the week ate it”.

A matching signal suggests a play; you start it. Its steps carry dates measured from the day you started, and overdue ones appear on Today until they are done. Five plays ship: re-engagement, pace recovery, champion change, unblock, graduation readiness.

Nothing starts on its own, and closing one asks for a verdict — worked, no effect, no longer relevant. That verdict stays on the account, because “we tried this in June and it did nothing” is the most useful thing to know before trying again.

After the runway

Did it actually hold?

The tracker measured onboarding all the way to graduation and then stopped caring, which meant it could tell you that you finished but never whether it worked.

Graduating an account now schedules one question, thirty days later: are they still doing the thing you onboarded them onto? Three answers, because five would be false precision on a judgement call. It reaches Today on its due date even though graduated accounts otherwise leave your queue.

The share of your graduations that held is the only figure in the tracker that measures the outcome of the work rather than its volume. It reports nothing at all until you have answered one — an empty denominator is not a score.

Commercial context

Value breaks ties. It never outranks urgency.

Every ranked list used to sort on execution quality alone, so a large account and a small one with the same health ranked identically. Settings → Import from Gainsight (CSV) brings in tier, contract value and renewal date — export whatever columns you have, map them, and review every change before anything is written.

Value then breaks ties inside a severity band and never crosses one. A burning small account still outranks a healthy large one. Urgency is what is on fire; value is which fire you walk to first.

The renewal date also unlocks a sentence the tracker could never say: this is projected to finish onboarding after the thing it was bought for comes up for renewal. It stays silent on any account whose pace is assumed rather than measured.

Copilot

Cards you can turn over

Copilot used to list accounts as name chips. A chip tells you an account is implicated and then makes you leave the tab to do anything about it.

Each list is now a horizontal rail of cards. Drag it and the figure behind each card drifts against the motion — that number is real: days to projected graduation, open risks, sessions completed. Flip a card and the fix for the thing that put it in that list is on the back: a graduation date, an outcome plan, a CSAT contact, a readiness note, a risk verdict.

The back names the account, the rail keeps your place when the page re-renders, and if your edit moves the card out of that list the tracker says so by name rather than letting it vanish.

Upstream

The half of the book nobody was watching

Accounts in partner-led implementation are deliberately kept out of the advisor: they are not chased for sessions and Gainsight gates they do not have yet. That is correct, and it had a side effect — nearly half a book can become invisible.

Still building on Copilot groups them by phase and orders them by silence, with days-in-phase measured from entering that phase rather than from the build start.

It notifies without nagging: two counts in the readout at the top of the tab, seen every time you open it, and no Today item at all. The quiet threshold is its own setting and much longer than a live account’s — three weeks of silence from an onboarding account is a problem; from a partner-led build it is a Tuesday.

What you owe

Questions they asked and you have not answered

An unanswered customer question used to be the most invisible thing in the tracker. It lived on the activity that raised it, rendered in exactly one place, and could never rank an account into your day — so an account whose only problem was that you owed them an answer was silent.

Owed an answer now opens the week on Copilot, oldest question first. Type the answer on the card back, or draft one with AI and read it before you send it.

They still do not compete with a stall for your attention. Whether an unanswered question should outrank a slipping account is a real judgement, and it has not been made for you.

Copilot

It opens on a door, not on five chapters

Copilot used to open straight into dense reading, every time. It now lands on the band, one computed sentence about the book, and a door.

The door is free. It never checks for an API key, because a tab that goes blank when a key expires is a tab you stop trusting. Open the read and you get the tracker’s own analysis of your week, month, builds and next actions.

Ask AI to read it adds a layer on top: one request, four lenses, written from a customer-success and project-management perspective. Only pseudonymous ids leave your browser — the real names are put back on screen afterwards, in your browser, so you can actually read it.

Copilot

Who to bring in, and when to hand over

What to do next is no longer a list of Gainsight chores — those moved to Today, where standing record-keeping belongs. It answers, per account: why this needs you, what the act is, and which specialist owns the answer.

The routing reads what the customer actually wrote. A methodology question routes to a Climate Consultant; a roadmap or defect question to a Product Success Manager; “how was our workspace configured” to the partner team; integrations to a Solution Architect. A pattern of concerning language across several notes routes to the Account Executive — never a single mention.

It will also suggest starting the CSM handoff early, on two roads that both need evidence: an account you have already chased through a re-engagement play that failed, or a multi-solution account that is nearly finished, healthy and owes you nothing.

Protect it

The backup that keeps itself current

Reminders are not a backup. A cleared profile, a reimaged laptop or a wiped browser profile does not warn you first.

Settings → Automatic backup. Pick a file once and the tracker writes your book to it whenever anything changes. Same contents as Export JSON, so it restores through Import JSON with no special handling — and no Downloads folder full of dated copies.

It costs one click per session, not one per backup: browsers will not hold write permission to your disk across sessions without a gesture. The header says which state it is in, and stops nagging you once it is live. Chrome only for now; everywhere else the old export path is unchanged.

Your call

Motion, and the switch that turns it down

Risk and Copilot move. The deck leans slightly with how fast you are scrolling and settles when you stop, chapter headings rise from behind a clipping edge, and the rail rows and the two gate buttons lean a few pixels toward your cursor. It is meant to make a dense tool feel like it has weight rather than to be looked at.

If it is not what you want while you are working, turn it down. Settings → Motion has two positions. Full is what you get by default. Calm keeps everything structural — the deck, the rail, the parallax, the instruments — and drops the skew, the reveals and the magnetic hover. Nothing is hidden either way; Calm is simply still.

The setting lives in your browser rather than in the book, so it does not travel with an export and it does not affect anyone else. If your system is set to reduce motion, Calm is used regardless of what the switch says — the switch can quiet things down but it can never turn motion on against your own accessibility setting.

Do not game the runway

Five shortcuts
that corrupt the truth.

Health is not a performance grade.

Version compatibility

Guide and tracker move as one package.

This guide is validated against the bundled tracker. Bug-fix releases should update the manifest and revalidate every instructional statement before distribution.

Both halves check themselves. Open the browser console and run runSelfChecks() on the tracker or runGuideSelfChecks() here. Each prints every assertion it made and must come back with none failed. They are how the two files are verified against each other before anyone relies on them — and they are read-only: the tracker’s suite ends by proving it left your book exactly as it found it, down to the last key.
